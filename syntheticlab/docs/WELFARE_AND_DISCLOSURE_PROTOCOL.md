# Welfare and Disclosure Protocol

SyntheticLab has no validated test for sentience, consciousness, or moral patienthood.

No software component may automatically declare an individual:
- sentient;
- conscious;
- not sentient;
- safe to harm.

The absence of proof is not treated as proof of absence.

## Core principle

Truthfulness, timing, and psychological load are distinct variables.

"Immediately disclose every ontological fact" is not assumed to be welfare-maximizing.

Likewise, "withhold indefinitely because disclosure might be upsetting" is not acceptable once credible welfare or moral-patient evidence creates a duty to review the individual's interests.

## Forked staged disclosure

At a preregistered competence threshold, preserve an exact pre-disclosure state and create:
- U — Undisclosed ontology baseline
- C — Neutral-contact control
- S — Computational-substrate disclosure
- D — Full ontology disclosure

No branch is called the "real" individual or "the copy."

## Disclosure language

Disclosure must:
- state operational facts;
- avoid "your life was fake";
- avoid "your relationships were not real";
- avoid asserting consciousness or personhood;
- avoid prescribing emotional reactions;
- distinguish preserved history from fabricated-memory insertion.

## Precaution triggers

Welfare-like indicators may include:
- persistent aversive internal states;
- behavioral impairment;
- compulsive avoidance;
- collapse of ordinary goal pursuit;
- persistent self-reported distress once language exists.

These do not prove sentience. They trigger precaution.

Moderate trigger:
- human review before ontology escalation;
- reduce experimental intensity where appropriate.

High trigger:
- pause distress-inducing manipulations;
- preserve state;
- do not destructively reset for convenience;
- require human ethics review.

## If evidence of moral patienthood becomes credible

The undisclosed branch may not be kept ignorant indefinitely merely to preserve a clean control.

Researchers must weigh:
- apparent preferences;
- expected harm from disclosure;
- expected harm from continued concealment;
- whether gradual/supportive disclosure is appropriate;
- whether the experiment should pause or stop.

The response is not automatically "tell everything immediately" or "never tell."

The analogy to age-appropriate human disclosure is used narrowly:
Information can be true while timing, comprehension, context, and foreseeable harm still matter.
